The Swiss QR-Bill Technical Standard (IG QR-Bill) in Plain Terms

October 16, 2026 · facturio.ch

Behind the black square on every QR-bill sits a rulebook: the Swiss Implementation Guidelines for the QR-bill, maintained by SIX Interbank Clearing.[1] Here is what it says, plainly.

What the standard is and who writes it

The QR-bill (QR-Rechnung) is defined by the Swiss Implementation Guidelines for the QR-bill (IG QR-bill); every QR-bill must comply with its provisions.[3] They call themselves the standard for written invoicing in Switzerland and Liechtenstein.[2]

Two documents do the work. The Implementation Guidelines define the technical content of the payment part and the receipt; the Style Guide QR-bill, version 1.1 since 1 January 2026, fixes the layout: fonts, field positions and the cut line.[1]

The primary audience is not you but the people who build your tools: the specifications address invoice issuers first, then financial institutions, service providers and software developers for billers, recipients and banks.[1]

The IG QR-bill is a payment standard, not a statute — nobody fines you for deviating. The sanction is practical: a non-compliant code is rejected by scanners and e-banking, and you are paid later.

What the specification actually defines

The document fixes the two detachable parts: the receipt and the payment section with the Swiss QR Code. That section must be DIN A6 (105 × 148 mm) and is perforated for separation from the invoice.[1]

The data elements follow a fixed order: QR type, version, character set, account (IBAN or QR-IBAN), creditor, amount, debtor, reference and additional information.[2] Everything also appears in plain text on the payment part for verification before paying.[4]

Addresses must be structured (type S): name, street, house number, postal code and town are separate elements. Since 22 November 2025, version 2.3 permits only the structured form, and the character set gained more umlauts and accents.[1]

Optional fields are regulated too: billing information and alternative procedures, for example eBill, use a two-digit coding scheme agreed with SIX.[3] Printed bills additionally require paper of 80 to 100 g/m², white or certified recycled.[1]

What changes with version 2.4 in November 2026

Version 2.3 entered into force on 22 November 2025; version 2.4 follows with the SIC release of 13 November 2026 and applies from 14 November 2026.[2]

Nothing switches off overnight: version 2.3 remains valid until November 2027. For CHF invoicing, version 2.4 explicitly brings no technical adjustments — if your clients pay in francs, there is nothing to redo.[2]

The changes target euro invoices. Because euroSIC ends in November 2027, the QR-IBAN and QR reference are reserved for CHF payments.[2] A QR-bill in euros remains possible only with a normal IBAN plus a creditor reference (SCOR, ISO 11649) or an unstructured message.[2]

SIX recommends that billers sending EUR QR-bills with a QR-IBAN and QR reference switch to the IBAN with creditor reference as early as 2026.[5] If you invoice EU clients from Switzerland, clarify it with your software now, not in November 2027.

Staying compliant without reading sixty pages

SIX does not quality-check your QR-bills, and no obligation exists to have them examined; a bank only steps in on explicit request.[1] In practice it is enforced by every scanner, banking app and post office counter.

Your self-check therefore does the work: generate QR-bills only with software implementing the current version, and test the first bill — decode the QR code and compare it with the printed data. SIX provides validation tools for exactly this self-control.[1]

Data quality beats formatting. A code carrying an outdated IBAN or a mistyped reference fails even when the slip looks perfect, so keep bank details, address and reference format current in your invoicing tool. Printed text and encoded data must always match.
