Selling Gift Vouchers (Gutscheine) in Switzerland: When MWST Applies

October 25, 2026 · facturio.ch

Pre-Christmas weeks are voucher season: a customer pays 50 francs at the till and walks out with a card promising 50 francs of your goods or services. Nothing has been supplied yet — and the ESTV treats that difference as decisive: Gutscheine are pure means of payment, their sale is not taxable, and MWST arises only on redemption.[2]

The voucher sale is not a supply

The ESTV's wording is blunt: Gutscheine are reine Zahlungsmittel — pure means of payment. Selling one performs no supply, so the amount taken is a Nicht-Entgelt, a non-consideration that is not taxable.[2]

That matches the statute: the tax is calculated on the consideration actually received,[1] and at the voucher counter no consideration for a supply has arrived — only cash against a promise.

The booking follows: on sale, cash is set against Gutscheine, a passive account — no revenue, no MWST owed.[2] The 50 francs are a debt you owe the holder until the card comes back.

The payoff is real: voucher sales never inflate taxable turnover.[2] Watch the CHF 100,000 registration threshold or plan with Saldosteuersatz figures — unredeemed cards must not appear as Umsatz in either.

Redemption is the tax event

The clock starts when the holder spends the card: at redemption, the Entgelt is taxed at the applicable rate — the value of the goods or services actually received, regardless of your Abrechnungsart.[2]

The rate follows the basket, not the voucher. A card spent on standard-rated goods carries the standard rate; spent on reduced-rated items, the reduced rate.[2] One voucher can feed several tax-rate lines in the same return, split by what the till recorded.

Booking runs in two steps: the redemption releases the Gutscheine liability — MWST-unwirksam — while the sale is booked as turnover with its tax.[2] The liability dissolves exactly as the taxable revenue arises.

Mixed payments stay ordinary: if the basket exceeds the card, the cash top-up is consideration too; if it costs less, the balance remains a liability until spent. A voucher delays the assessment of a sale — it never changes it.

Never show MWST on the voucher receipt

The disclosure rule is explicit: receipts, invoices or confirmations for voucher sales must not carry a tax disclosure, because the sale lies outside VAT.[2]

Art. 27 MWSTG gives that rule teeth: whoever details too high a tax — or tax where none is due — owes the tax shown, unless the invoice is corrected or the Confederation is shown to have suffered no loss.[1] A tax-printed voucher receipt converts the holder's 50 francs into unearned turnover.

Keep the voucher receipt factual: date, amount, voucher number, validity and redemption conditions. The formal invoice rules bite only once a real supply is invoiced — the ESTV anchors the disclosure ban in its Buchführung und Rechnungsstellung rules.[2][4]

The redemption receipt is a different animal: it documents an actual sale. Till receipts up to 400 francs need no recipient details — though they never entitle the holder to a tax refund in the refund procedure.[3]

Expiry, e-purses and books that survive an audit

The Nicht-Entgelt principle has a quiet corollary: a never-redeemed voucher never triggers a supply, so no MWST ever arises on it. Releasing an expired card's balance is a bookkeeping step, not a tax event — the amount was never turnover.

Electronic purses ride the same track: loading a card or key performs no supply, and the tax falls due only as the credit is spent — whether or not you are also the issuer.[2]

Discipline makes it auditable: issue vouchers with serial numbers, keep a ledger of sold, redeemed and outstanding balances, and reconcile the Gutscheine account against your till records. A MwSt-Kontrolle will happily walk exactly that path.
